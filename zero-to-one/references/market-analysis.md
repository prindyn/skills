# Market Analysis Reference

Frameworks for evaluating market opportunity through a Zero to One lens. Use this when assessing a specific startup idea's market dynamics, competitive landscape, or long-term defensibility.

---

## The Monopoly Evaluation Checklist

Score each dimension from 0–3. A strong idea should reach at least 7 total, with no dimension scoring 0.

### 1. Proprietary Technology (0–3)
- **0** — Technology is the same or marginally better than existing alternatives
- **1** — Technology is meaningfully better (2–5× improvement) in a specific dimension
- **2** — Technology is 10× better in one important dimension
- **3** — Technology creates a new capability that previously didn't exist at any price

Questions to probe:
- If a competitor allocated 10× the engineering resources, how long would it take them to match this?
- Is the advantage from hard-to-replicate invention, or from execution that anyone could copy with time and money?
- Is the 10× improvement on a dimension customers actually care about (vs. a metric that looks impressive internally)?

### 2. Network Effects (0–3)
- **0** — Product is equally useful with 1 user or 1 million users
- **1** — Product becomes somewhat more useful with more users (data network effects, weak social graph)
- **2** — Core value proposition depends on critical mass in a well-defined group
- **3** — Multi-sided network where each side grows the other (marketplace, platform, protocol)

Questions to probe:
- What is the minimum viable network — the smallest group where the product is already useful?
- Is there a defensible reason users can't defect to a rival network once the network is established?
- Does the network effect compound over time, or does it plateau?

### 3. Economies of Scale (0–3)
- **0** — Costs scale linearly with revenue (each new customer costs roughly the same to serve)
- **1** — Some fixed cost leverage (e.g., shared infrastructure) but significant variable costs remain
- **2** — Strong unit economics improvement at scale; gross margins expand meaningfully with growth
- **3** — Near-zero marginal cost per additional user (software, data products, content platforms)

Questions to probe:
- What is the gross margin today, and what does it look like at 10×, 100× current volume?
- Are there structural costs that never go away, regardless of scale (e.g., regulations, physical infrastructure)?
- Can the business maintain quality while scaling, or does quality degrade with volume?

### 4. Branding (0–3)
- **0** — No coherent brand identity; customers see it as a commodity or generic service
- **1** — Distinct visual or verbal identity, but nothing that meaningfully differentiates in the customer's mind
- **2** — Strong brand that signals specific values and attracts a defined customer type
- **3** — Brand constitutes a category of its own; competitors are framed as inferior imitations

Questions to probe:
- What does a customer feel when they describe this product to a friend? Pride, utility, indifference?
- Is the brand built on real substantive difference, or does it substitute for one?
- Would the brand survive if the founders left or the aesthetics were changed?

## The Seven Questions Score Sheet

Rate each of Thiel's seven questions for any given idea. If any question gets "no," understand exactly why before proceeding.

| Question | Key test | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| **Engineering** | Is the core technology 10× better? | "We're combining existing technologies" without a clear 10× claim |
| **Timing** | Why is now the right moment? | Can't identify a recent change that made this newly possible |
| **Monopoly** | Do you own a specific small market? | "We'll go after 1% of a huge market" |
| **People** | Are founders deeply connected to the problem? | Founders building for a customer they don't know and a problem they haven't lived |
| **Distribution** | How do you reach first 100 customers specifically? | "We'll grow virally" or "We'll do content marketing" with no concrete path |
| **Durability** | Is this defensible in 10–20 years? | All moats can be bypassed by larger players with more resources |
| **Secret** | What do you see that others don't? | The opportunity is widely recognized; many well-funded startups are already pursuing it |

## Competitive Landscape Analysis

### Mapping the real market

Avoid both monopolist and competitor framing errors:

**Competitor error (non-monopolist):** Defining the market as the intersection of many narrow attributes to appear unique.
- Example: "We're the only British restaurant in downtown Palo Alto" (true but the real market is Palo Alto restaurants, not British restaurants)
- Corrected: "How do we compare to all restaurants customers would consider instead of us?"

**Monopolist error:** Defining the market as a union of many large markets to appear small.
- Example: "We're just one small player in the global $495B advertising market" (technically true of Google's search ad business)
- Corrected: "What is the specific market where we have pricing power today?"

The honest question: **In what specific market can this company set its own prices?**

### Competitive moat assessment

For each existing competitor or potential entrant, assess:

| Factor | This company | Best competitor |
|---|---|---|
| Technology advantage | | |
| Distribution advantage | | |
| Brand recognition | | |
| Network size | | |
| Switching costs for customers | | |
| Data advantage | | |
| Regulatory position | | |

Where this company is significantly stronger, that's a moat. Where competitors are stronger, that's a vulnerability.

### The "Don't Disrupt" test

A startup that defines itself primarily by its opposition to existing companies is in trouble. Ask:
- Is the core pitch "we're better than [incumbent]" or "we do something they can't do at all"?
- If the incumbent copied the product, would customers move? (If yes, the moat isn't strong enough.)
- Is the startup creating new value in the market, or just redistributing existing value?

Companies that fight incumbents directly — especially large ones — tend to underperform because they let incumbents define the terms of competition.

## Market Sequencing Framework

### The progression model

| Phase | Market size | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| Beachhead | Hundreds to thousands of users | 100% of a tiny, specific market |
| Early expansion | Tens of thousands | 20–40% of adjacent markets |
| Scale | Millions | Dominant position in large category |

### Evaluating adjacency

For each proposed expansion market, verify:
1. **Shared customer** — The same people who used the product in Phase 1 are the target in Phase 2
2. **Shared distribution** — You can reach Phase 2 customers through relationships or channels built in Phase 1
3. **Shared technology** — The core technical capability from Phase 1 applies to Phase 2 with adaptation, not replacement
4. **Earned credibility** — Phase 1 success makes Phase 2 customers trust you

If none of these links exist, the expansion is a discontinuous leap — harder to execute and less likely to succeed.

### Sequencing anti-patterns

- **Too big too fast:** Launching in a large market without a defensible niche. Funds spent acquiring customers who will leave for incumbents with better distribution.
- **Stuck in the niche:** The niche is profitable but there is no natural path to scale. The business becomes a lifestyle business, not a venture-scale company.
- **Wrong order:** Expanding to market B before truly dominating market A. Competitors move into market A while attention is divided.

## Cash Flow and Business Durability

A startup's value is the sum of all money it will make in the future, discounted to present value.

Key implications:
- **High growth + low near-term profits** can be more valuable than **moderate growth + strong near-term profits** if the high-growth business has a longer runway of compounding cash flows
- **Old Economy businesses** (restaurants, newspapers) have most of their value in near-term cash flows — which plateau or decline
- **Tech businesses with network effects** have most of their value 10–15 years out — which is why patient capital and long-term planning are essential

Questions to assess durability:
- What would the business look like in 10 years if it succeeds? What are customers paying for, and why would they still pay?
- What is the primary threat to long-term value: (a) better technology, (b) regulation, (c) customer behavior change, (d) a better-funded competitor?
- Is the company building toward the "last mover" position — the final great development in its niche — or is it just another iteration that will itself be iterated over?
