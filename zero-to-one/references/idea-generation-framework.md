# Idea Generation Framework

Structured prompts, exercises, and patterns for generating 0-to-1 startup ideas. Use this when the user wants help generating ideas from scratch or when the brainstorming needs more structure.

---

## Part 1: Excavating Secrets

The best startup ideas are built on secrets — truths that exist but haven't been widely discovered. Use these exercises to surface them.

### Exercise A: The Frustration Audit
Ask the user to list three things they deal with regularly that are broken, slow, or unnecessarily painful — things that others seem to have accepted as normal. For each:
- Who else shares this frustration?
- Why hasn't someone solved it?
- Is the obstacle technical, regulatory, social, or just inertia?

Good secrets often hide behind "everyone knows this is broken" — because knowing something is broken and actually solving it are very different.

### Exercise B: The Expert Blind Spot
Pick an industry or field the user knows well. Now ask:
- What do experts in this field believe that is actually wrong or outdated?
- What do practitioners do every day that they can't explain with a principled reason?
- What would an intelligent outsider find bizarre about how this industry operates?

Industries with strong professional cultures (medicine, law, finance, academia) tend to accumulate unjustified orthodoxies that are ripe for disruption by people willing to question them.

### Exercise C: The Underserved Person
Identify a group of people who are poorly served by current products or services:
- People too poor to afford the professional version (what's their equivalent of "bank for the unbanked"?)
- People in a niche that's too small for large companies to care about (yet)
- People in an early stage of a demographic shift (aging populations, new immigrant communities, remote workers)
- People whose preferences have changed faster than the products they use

Ask: what would a product look like if it were designed specifically for these people — not as an afterthought, not as a price-reduced version of something else?

### Exercise D: The Technology Transfer
Pick a proven technology from one domain and ask where it hasn't been applied yet:

| Source domain | Target domain to explore |
|---|---|
| Machine learning applied to consumer apps | Scientific research, industrial processes, professional services |
| Software's zero-marginal-cost model | Physical goods distribution |
| Network effects from social media | B2B tools, local services, niche communities |
| Mobile ubiquity | Sectors still assuming desktop access |
| AI language models | Specialized professional workflows |

The secret here is that breakthrough applications often come from people who understand the technology deeply but aren't captured by the conventions of the target domain.

### Exercise E: The Timing Question
Some ideas are right but early — the market or technology wasn't ready. Others are newly possible because something changed. Ask:
- What became technically feasible in the last 2–3 years that wasn't before?
- What regulatory change recently opened a market?
- What behavioral shift from recent events (remote work, aging demographics, climate awareness) created new needs that old products don't serve?
- What cost has dropped dramatically (compute, genomics, sensors, batteries) that enables new businesses?

## Part 2: Idea Generation Patterns

These are repeating patterns that have produced successful 0-to-1 businesses. Use them as generators, not formulas.

### Pattern 1: Take the Offline to Online — but for real this time
Not "Uber for X" or "Airbnb for Y." Look for categories where the offline-to-online transition *hasn't actually happened* despite years of attempts. Ask: why did previous attempts fail? Was it a trust problem, a logistics problem, a technology problem? Has that obstacle been removed?

### Pattern 2: The Professional Tool for the Amateur
Professionals in any field have access to powerful tools that amateurs cannot use — because they're too complex, too expensive, or require training. As the amateur population for a skill grows (cooking, investing, health tracking, music production, video editing), there is a window to build the prosumer version: simpler, cheaper, but genuinely powerful.

### Pattern 3: The Hidden Aggregation Opportunity
Fragmented industries with many small providers and no dominant platform often have room for one aggregator who creates the coordination layer. The secret: the aggregator doesn't need to own the supply. It just needs to make discovery, comparison, or transaction meaningfully easier than the fragmented status quo.

### Pattern 4: Fixing a Broken Incentive Structure
Some industries remain dysfunctional because the people making decisions aren't the ones who bear the costs. Healthcare, insurance, legal services, real estate — in each, the decision-maker and the cost-bearer are often misaligned. A business that realigns these incentives (by changing who pays, or how they pay) can unlock enormous value.

### Pattern 5: The New Infrastructure for a Maturing Market
When a new category of activity explodes in scale, it needs infrastructure that didn't exist before: payments, compliance, analytics, tooling, talent marketplaces. The pickaxe sellers during the gold rush often made more money than the miners. Ask: what is scaling so fast that its infrastructure is creaking?

### Pattern 6: The Institutional Problem Solved by Software
Large institutions (hospitals, universities, government agencies, banks) use software that was built decades ago and hasn't been meaningfully replaced. The software is bad; switching costs are high; vendors have no incentive to improve. The opportunity: build something genuinely better and find the entry point that makes switching compelling.

## Part 3: Filtering Ideas

Once you have 3–5 candidate ideas, apply these filters to rank them.

### Filter 1: The Surprise Test
Describe the idea to a smart, experienced person in the relevant industry. If they immediately say "of course, great idea" — that's a bad sign. The best ideas produce a reaction of "hm, I hadn't thought of it that way" or mild skepticism. If everyone immediately loves it, too many people will try to do it.

### Filter 2: The Founder-Market Fit Test
Does the person building this have special insight, access, or credibility that others don't? Outsiders often fail in industries where trust and domain knowledge matter. But outsiders often succeed where insiders are too captured by existing assumptions. Ask: what is this specific founder's unfair advantage here?

### Filter 3: The Endgame Test
Project 10 years forward. If the company succeeds, what does it look like? Does it have pricing power? Are its customers locked in by switching costs, habit, or network effects? Is the core technology still proprietary? A business that can only succeed for 3 years before being commoditized is not a great business.

### Filter 4: The Distribution Test
Who are the first 100 customers? How do you reach them specifically? Is there a natural community, channel, or event where they concentrate? "We'll do SEO and content marketing" is not a distribution strategy for an early-stage startup. You need a direct path to the first cohort.

### Filter 5: The Team Test
Who needs to be in the room to build this? If the idea requires capabilities the current team doesn't have and can't easily acquire, either build the team first or consider whether the idea is right for this team.

## Part 4: From Idea to Starting Market

Even a great idea fails if launched into the wrong initial market. Use this framework to select the beachhead.

**Criteria for a good starting market:**
- Small enough to dominate in 12–24 months (hundreds to thousands of customers, not millions)
- Specific enough to have a clear shared identity (a profession, a community, a geography, a use case)
- Underserved enough that even a V1 product is meaningfully better than the status quo
- Adjacent to larger markets that can be reached in subsequent expansions

**Mapping the expansion path:**
Draw a map from the starting niche to the eventual large market. Each step should share one of:
- The same customer (different job-to-be-done)
- The same channel (different product sold through the same relationship)
- The same technology (applied to a different problem)

If the path from niche to scale requires a discontinuous leap — a completely different customer, channel, and technology — reconsider the starting market.

**Common mistakes in market selection:**
- Picking a large market because it sounds impressive (leaves you fighting entrenched competitors for scraps)
- Picking a market you can't own even at small scale (no real differentiation from existing niche players)
- Picking a market that doesn't connect to anything larger (a permanent niche with no expansion path)
