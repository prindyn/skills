# Red Flags Cheatsheet — Bad Data Patterns to Catch in Real Time

Keep this list handy during and after customer conversations. These are the patterns that feel like validation but aren't.

---

## During the Conversation

### Red Flag 1: They're Complimenting, Not Committing

**What it sounds like:**
- "That's a really cool idea."
- "I love this — you should definitely build it."
- "I could see this being super useful."
- "Wow, this could be really big."

**Why it's a red flag:** Compliments are given freely and forgotten immediately. They cost the speaker nothing. When someone compliments your idea, they are being polite (or genuinely enthusiastic), but they are not telling you whether the underlying problem is real or important enough to pay for.

**What to do:** Thank them, then redirect. "I'm glad it resonates. I'm curious about your experience with [problem area] — tell me about the last time this actually came up for you."

---

### Red Flag 2: They're Talking About the Future, Not the Past

**What it sounds like:**
- "I would definitely use something like that."
- "In the future, I could see needing this."
- "Once we grow a bit, this would make a lot of sense."
- "Next quarter we're planning to tackle this."

**Why it's a red flag:** People are reliably optimistic about their hypothetical future selves. Future-tense statements are predictions about behavior, not evidence of it. The person may genuinely believe they'd use it — and still never sign up.

**What to do:** Anchor them in the past. "Has anything like this come up already? What did you do?" or "What have you done so far to prepare for that?"

---

### Red Flag 3: They're Giving Generic, Third-Person Opinions

**What it sounds like:**
- "Yeah, this is a huge problem in the industry."
- "Everyone I know deals with this."
- "It's a well-known pain point."
- "My whole team would use this."

**Why it's a red flag:** Third-person statements shift the claim away from the speaker. They can't be verified, and they often dissolve under a single follow-up question. "Everyone" rarely means everyone.

**What to do:** Pull it personal. "Have *you* personally experienced this?" or "Tell me about the last time *you* dealt with it."

---

### Red Flag 4: They're Enthusiastic but Vague About the Problem

**What it sounds like:**
- "Absolutely, this is a real pain point." (No specifics follow.)
- "We struggle with this all the time." (No examples given.)
- "This is something everyone wants to improve." (No concrete description of what "improve" means.)

**Why it's a red flag:** Enthusiasm without specificity is noise. A real problem leaves tracks: stories, examples, failed workarounds, money spent, time lost. If they can't name a specific instance, the problem may be theoretical.

**What to do:** Ask for a specific story. "Can you walk me through the last time this happened? What were you actually doing, and what went wrong?"

---

### Red Flag 5: They're Idea-Mode (Designing Features, Not Describing Problems)

**What it sounds like:**
- "You know what would be really cool? If you could also…"
- "I'd want it to integrate with [tool]."
- "You should add a dashboard that shows…"

**Why it's a red flag:** When customers start designing features, they've switched from describing their reality to imagining a product. This is often a sign they're being helpful rather than truthful. Feature requests from people who don't have the problem urgently are not validation.

**What to do:** Appreciate it, then ground it. "That's interesting — can you tell me how you handle [that thing] today? I want to understand the current pain before thinking about solutions."

---

### Red Flag 6: The Meeting Ended With No Clear Next Step

**What it sounds like:**
- "Well, thanks for sharing — keep me updated!"
- "Let me know when you launch."
- "This is great, I'll think about it."
- "Feel free to reach out anytime."

**Why it's a red flag:** A warm but commitment-free close means you haven't established that the problem is urgent enough to act on. They're being polite, not signaling intent.

**What to do (in the moment):** Ask explicitly. "Would you be willing to be part of a small group testing an early version? It'd be maybe 30 minutes of your time over a few weeks." The answer tells you more than the whole conversation before it.

---

### Red Flag 7: They Asked About Features Before Confirming the Problem

**What it sounds like:**
- "Does it have X?"
- "Will it work with Y?"
- "Can you do Z?"

**Why it's a red flag:** Feature questions before problem confirmation mean you've started pitching before learning. You've shifted the conversation into evaluation mode before you know if there's a genuine fit.

**What to do:** Recognize this means you shared your idea too early. In future conversations, stay in discovery mode longer before revealing the solution.

---

## After the Conversation

### Red Flag 8: You Only Have Compliments and No Quotes

If you review your notes and you have:
- Multiple "they seemed excited" notes
- No verbatim quotes about a specific past experience
- No description of a workaround they've built
- No dollar or time figure attached to the problem

…you learned very little. The conversation was pleasant but not validating.

---

### Red Flag 9: You Can't Describe the Problem in Their Words

A useful test: Can you write a one-paragraph description of the problem using only language and examples from your customer conversations? If your description uses your own framing and your own vocabulary, you haven't absorbed their reality yet.

---

### Red Flag 10: All Your Data Points the Same Direction

Counterintuitively, if every single conversation has been positive with no friction, that's a warning sign. Either you're talking to a segment that isn't representative, or you're unconsciously asking questions that invite agreement. Introduce harder questions and see if the signal holds.

---

## Quick Sanity Check After Each Conversation

Score the conversation by asking yourself:

1. Did I learn anything that surprised me? (If no: you may have been seeking confirmation.)
2. Can I name a specific moment where they described real past behavior? (If no: it was mostly hypothetical.)
3. Did the conversation end with a concrete next step they committed to? (If no: it wasn't a commitment signal.)
4. Did I do less than 40% of the talking? (If no: I talked too much.)
5. Do I have at least one verbatim quote I'd feel confident sharing with a skeptical investor? (If no: keep digging.)
