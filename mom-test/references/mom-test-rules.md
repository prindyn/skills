# The Mom Test — Core Rules and Methodology

## Why "The Mom Test"?

Your mom loves you and will lie to protect your feelings. So will most people you pitch an idea to. The Mom Test asks: **could you run this conversation with your mom and still get true information?** If yes, you're asking the right questions.

The goal of customer discovery is **not** to get people to like your idea. It is to learn whether a real problem exists, how serious it is, and whether people are already trying to solve it.

---

## The Three Rules

### Rule 1: Talk About Their Life, Not Your Idea

The moment you describe your idea, you've changed the dynamic. Now the other person is being polite, giving feedback, or playing advisor. You're no longer learning — you're pitching.

**What to do instead:** Ask about their experience with the underlying problem space. What do they do today? How do they handle it? What's the hardest part?

Their life and their existing behavior are always truthful. Their reaction to your idea is almost always noisy.

**Never say:**
- "I'm building X, what do you think?"
- "Would you use something that did Y?"
- "Does this sound useful to you?"

**Do say:**
- "Walk me through the last time you dealt with [problem area]."
- "How do you handle [situation] today?"
- "What's the hardest part of [workflow]?"

---

### Rule 2: Ask About Specifics in the Past, Not Hypotheticals About the Future

"Would you ever…?" is the most dangerous question in product development. People are optimistic about hypothetical future versions of themselves. They will say yes to almost anything.

Past behavior is a reliable predictor. Future intent is not.

**Why hypotheticals lie:**
- People want to be helpful, so they say what sounds helpful.
- People genuinely believe they would use something even when they wouldn't.
- Enthusiasm in a conversation does not correlate with action after the conversation.

**What to ask instead:**

| Hypothetical (bad) | Past-behavior anchor (good) |
|---|---|
| "Would you pay for this?" | "How much did you spend on solving this last year?" |
| "Would you use a tool that did X?" | "What did you do last time X happened?" |
| "Do you think you'd find this helpful?" | "Have you ever tried to solve this? What happened?" |
| "Would you switch from your current solution?" | "What have you already tried? Why did it fall short?" |

---

### Rule 3: Talk Less, Listen More

You are there to extract information, not to impress or persuade. Every minute you spend talking is a minute you're not learning.

**Signs you're talking too much:**
- You've explained your idea in detail.
- You've answered objections before they're raised.
- The other person is nodding but not contributing new information.
- You're steering them toward a particular answer.

**Practices:**
- Ask a question, then stay quiet. Let silence do work.
- When they give a vague answer, say: "Can you tell me more about that?"
- When they give an interesting answer, say: "Can you give me a specific example?"
- Aim to speak less than 30% of the time.

## The Spectrum of Data Quality

Not all information from a conversation is equally useful. From least to most valuable:

### Fluff (discard or heavily discount)

- Generic opinions: "Yeah, I think that's a real problem."
- Hypothetical futures: "I would definitely use that."
- Vague references: "People are always frustrated with that kind of thing."
- Compliments: "That's a great idea, you should build it."

Fluff feels good in the moment. It teaches you nothing.

### Facts (keep, weight by specificity)

- Past behavior: "Last month I had to manually copy 200 rows between spreadsheets."
- Current workarounds: "We use a shared Google Doc and a Slack channel to coordinate."
- Spent resources: "We pay $800/month for a tool that almost does this."
- Frequency data: "This happens every time we onboard a new client, so maybe twice a week."

Facts describe *what is happening*, not *what might happen*.

### Commitments (highest value — treat as votes)

Real commitment means something is at stake for them — time, money, or reputation.

- **Time commitment:** "Can I sit in on your next sales call to see how this actually comes up?"
- **Money commitment:** "Here's $200 to reserve early access."
- **Reputation commitment:** "I can intro you to the CFO at my company — she deals with this constantly."
- **Letter of intent / pilot agreement**

A commitment is the closest thing to a real signal you'll get before you ship.

## The Importance of Emotion and Context

Numbers and facts are useful. But the emotional texture of a problem is what tells you whether it's *important enough to fix*.

Listen for:
- **Frustration:** "It drives me crazy that…"
- **Resignation:** "We've just accepted that this is how it works."
- **Workarounds as evidence:** If someone has built a homemade solution, the problem is real.
- **Unsolicited mentions:** If they bring up the problem without you asking, it's top of mind.

A customer who says "it's a bit annoying sometimes" has a different problem than one who says "we lost a client because of this." Both are facts. The weight is very different.

---

## What You Are Not Trying to Do

- You are **not** trying to sell them on your idea.
- You are **not** trying to validate that you had a good idea.
- You are **not** trying to make them feel heard so they'll take the meeting.
- You are **not** collecting opinions.

You are trying to **learn the shape of a real problem** well enough to know whether your proposed solution could solve it — or whether you need to pivot.

---

## The Conversation Posture

The best customer discovery conversations feel like a **casual catch-up with a curious friend**, not a formal interview or a sales call.

- Keep it conversational, not transactional.
- Be genuinely curious — don't fake it.
- It's fine to say "I'm early-stage and still figuring out if this is a real problem."
- You can share context about what you're exploring *after* you've pulled all the information you need — not before.
