# Commitment Signals — What Real Validation Looks Like

## The Core Distinction

Interest is free. Commitment costs something.

When someone says "I'd love that" or "sounds great," it tells you almost nothing. They might genuinely mean it. But saying it costs them nothing — no time, no money, no social capital. That's why it's not signal.

**A real commitment means something is at stake for the other person:** their time, their money, or their reputation. When they commit, they are implicitly saying "I believe in this enough to put skin in the game."

---

## The Three Types of Commitment

### 1. Time Commitment

They give you something that competes with everything else on their calendar.

**Examples:**
- "I'll set aside an hour for you to shadow me during our weekly planning session."
- "Let me introduce you to our ops team — I'll set up the call."
- "I can be part of your beta group. What's the onboarding process?"
- "Send me a prototype and I'll test it with my team next week."

**Why it matters:** Time is finite and protected. When someone offers real access — to their workflow, their team, or their process — they believe the problem you're solving is real.

---

### 2. Money Commitment

They exchange actual currency, a deposit, a letter of intent, or a signed agreement.

**Examples:**
- Pre-payment for early access or a founding plan
- A signed letter of intent (LOI) or pilot agreement
- Payment for a consulting engagement to solve the problem while you build
- "We've already budgeted $X for this — if your product works, that money is yours."

**Why it matters:** Money signals that the pain is real and that they've decided to act, not just to hope. A $50 deposit is worth more than 100 enthusiastic "yes, I'd pay for that" responses.

**Watch for:** Budget owners vs. budget advocates. The person you're talking to might love the idea, but if they don't control the budget, you're hearing from an enthusiast, not a buyer. Ask: "Who makes this decision at your organization?"

---

### 3. Reputation Commitment

They put their professional credibility on the line by connecting you to others.

**Examples:**
- "Here's my colleague's direct email — I'll send her a note about you beforehand."
- "I'll mention this to our CEO next week. She's been looking for something like this."
- "I'm going to send a message to our industry Slack group — this is something people ask about all the time."

**Why it matters:** Making an introduction is a social risk. People protect their reputation. If they're willing to attach their name to your early, unproven product, they genuinely believe in it.

---

## Advancement vs. Compliment

Every conversation ends in one of two places:

### Advancement (good)
A clear next step that moves you closer to real learning or real commitment.

- "Can I sit in on your next planning meeting?"
- "Send me the prototype — I'll test it with my team by Friday."
- "Can I be pilot customer #1?"
- "I'll intro you to three people who deal with this every day."

### Compliment / Non-Advancement (discard as signal)
Positive words with no action attached.

- "This sounds really interesting."
- "I'll think about it and let you know."
- "Keep me in the loop!"
- "Definitely, I'd use that."
- "You should talk to [vague person] sometime."

The test: **Has the conversation produced a concrete next step that the other person is committed to?** If not, the meeting was informational, not validating.

---

## Soft vs. Hard Commitments

Not all commitments are equal. Use this hierarchy:

| Signal | Weight |
|---|---|
| Signed agreement / pre-payment | Very high |
| Verbal agreement to a specific pilot with a date | High |
| Warm introduction to a named decision-maker | High |
| Agreement to test a prototype by a specific date | Medium-high |
| Agreement to a follow-up meeting with a specific agenda | Medium |
| "Keep me updated" / "Send me the link when it's live" | Low |
| "Sounds great, I'd definitely use it" | Discard as signal |

---

## How to Ask for Commitment Without Pitching

You don't have to reveal your full solution to get a commitment signal. You can ask:

- "Would you be willing to be part of a small group of early testers? It would probably be 30 minutes of your time over the next few weeks."
- "We're trying to understand this problem better. Would you be open to me observing how you handle [workflow] next time it comes up?"
- "We're doing a small paid pilot with a handful of early partners. If this solves the problem for you, would you be open to a conversation about that?"

If they say yes, that's signal. If they hedge or deflect, that's also signal.

---

## Reading Hesitation

Sometimes the most informative response isn't a yes or a no — it's the shape of their hesitation.

- "Let me check with my team" → budget owner likely isn't in the room
- "I'm really busy right now" → problem isn't high priority for them
- "We'd need to see it fully built first" → risk aversion, or they don't believe you'll deliver
- "Send me some more info" → you haven't made a case for why this conversation is worth their time

These aren't rejections. They're information about what it would actually take to get their commitment, which is useful for refining your approach or your segment.

---

## Recording Commitment Signals in Your Notes

When filling out `templates/interview-notes.md`, mark each commitment signal explicitly:

```
[COMMITMENT - TIME] Offered to be a beta tester, agreed to give 1hr/week
[COMMITMENT - MONEY] Mentioned existing $400/mo budget for tools like this
[COMMITMENT - REPUTATION] Said she'd intro me to the VP of Ops
[NON-ADVANCE] "Sounds great, keep me posted"
```

This makes it easy for the signal-classifier agent to triage your notes after the call.
