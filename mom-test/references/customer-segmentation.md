# Customer Segmentation — Finding the Right People to Talk To

## Why Segmentation Matters Before Interviews

If you talk to everyone, you learn about no one. "People who would use our app" is not a segment — it's a wish. "Mid-market SaaS operations managers who currently track vendor renewals in spreadsheets" is a segment. The difference matters enormously:

- Vague segments produce contradictory interview data.
- Specific segments let you find the same person five times in a row and triangulate.
- Specificity makes outreach far easier — you know where these people gather.

The goal of segmentation before customer discovery is to get narrow enough that you can reach people reliably and that the conversations are meaningfully comparable.

---

## The Segmentation Funnel

Start broad and narrow until you have a specific, reachable person.

### Level 1 — Domain (too broad to interview)

> "People who manage projects"
> "Small businesses"
> "Healthcare professionals"

These are categories, not segments. You can't find "small businesses" at a meetup.

---

### Level 2 — Use Case (still too broad)

> "Project managers who track deadlines manually"
> "Small restaurant owners who do their own bookkeeping"
> "Nurses who document patient notes during shift"

Better. You have a problem area. But you still don't know who to call.

---

### Level 3 — Specific Segment (ready to interview)

> "Operations managers at 20–200-person B2B SaaS companies who are responsible for vendor contract renewals and currently track them in Google Sheets or Notion"
> "Independent restaurant owners in cities with 1–3 locations who do their own QuickBooks and have been in business 2–5 years"
> "ICU nurses at teaching hospitals who document in Epic and take notes by hand during rounds"

At this level, you know:
- The role (job title / function)
- The company or organization context (size, type, stage)
- The specific workflow or problem you're investigating
- Their current approach (the thing they're already doing)

---

## The Segmentation Questions to Answer

Before starting interviews, answer these:

1. **What is the role or identity of the person?**
   (Job title, department, whether they're a founder/employee/freelancer, etc.)

2. **What is the context they're operating in?**
   (Company size, industry, geography, stage if a startup, etc.)

3. **What specific task or workflow are we investigating?**
   (Not "project management" — "the weekly sprint planning meeting where priorities get negotiated across teams")

4. **What is their current behavior in this area?**
   (What tool, process, or workaround do they currently use? This is your baseline.)

5. **Where do these people gather?**
   (Slack communities, LinkedIn groups, conferences, local meetups, Reddit threads, existing customer list, friends-of-friends)

## The Beachhead Principle

You cannot serve everyone at once. The purpose of segmentation isn't to exclude people forever — it's to find your beachhead: the **single slice of the market where you can win completely and quickly**, then use that foothold to expand.

A good beachhead segment:
- Has the problem acutely (they feel the pain, not just awareness of it)
- Is reachable (you know where to find them)
- Is small enough that you can dominate it (not just participate in it)
- Has money or time to spend on a solution
- Will give you referrals to similar people

## The Two-Dimensional Narrowing Technique

If you're struggling to narrow your segment, use this matrix:

| | **High frequency of problem** | **Low frequency of problem** |
|---|---|---|
| **High severity of problem** | Ideal beachhead ✓ | Explore further |
| **Low severity of problem** | Interesting but weak signal | Deprioritize |

Ask: who experiences this problem **most often** AND **most painfully**? That intersection is your starting segment.

## Common Segmentation Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Segmenting by demographics instead of behavior

"Women aged 25–40" is a demographic. "Women aged 25–40 who track their cycle and work remotely with unpredictable schedules" is closer to a behavior-based segment — and much more useful.

### Mistake 2: Starting too broad to avoid excluding potential customers

Counterintuitively, narrowing your segment increases your chances of finding real signal. You can always expand later once you understand the core problem.

### Mistake 3: Changing segments mid-research

If you talk to five different types of people, you'll get five different problems. Pick one segment for the first wave of interviews. After learning from them, you can decide whether to expand or pivot.

### Mistake 4: Assuming "anyone with the problem" is a segment

The problem you're solving may affect many different types of people differently. A $10/month consumer app and a $50k/year enterprise deal are different products, different sales motions, different validation processes. Be specific about which you're building for.

## Finding People in Your Segment

Once you have a specific segment, here's where to find them:

| Channel | Best for |
|---|---|
| Warm intros from network | Fastest, highest-quality access |
| Slack / Discord communities | B2B professionals, developers, niche interest groups |
| LinkedIn with specific search filters | Job titles, company size, industry |
| Reddit / forums | Consumer segments with shared identity |
| Your existing users or customers | Already know your space |
| Conferences and meetups | In-person access, high concentration |
| Cold email (very targeted) | Specific company/role, personalized message |
| Twitter / X | Thought leaders, early adopters |

**Warm intros beat everything.** If you have one good conversation, end it by asking: "Who else do you know who deals with this kind of thing? I'd love an intro."

---

## When to Expand Your Segment

After 5+ interviews within one segment, you'll know whether to stay or expand:

- **Stay** if: the problem is consistent, the severity is high, you're getting commitment signals
- **Expand** if: the segment is too small to build a business on, or the problem is weak for this group but might be stronger for an adjacent group
- **Pivot** if: the problem you assumed exists doesn't, but a different important problem is showing up across interviews
